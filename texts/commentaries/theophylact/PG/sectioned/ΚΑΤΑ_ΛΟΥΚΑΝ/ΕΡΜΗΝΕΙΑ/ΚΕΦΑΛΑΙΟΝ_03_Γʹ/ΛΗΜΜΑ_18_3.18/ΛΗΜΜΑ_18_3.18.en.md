# Luke 3 (Γʹ) — Lemma 18 · Luke 3:18

> **So then, exhorting them with many other things also, he preached good news to the people. (Luke 3:18)**

Three orders having come to him, John admonishes: the crowds, the tax collectors, and the soldiers. To the crowds, then, he exhorts to attend to almsgiving, commanding him who has two tunics to share with him who has not; but to the tax collectors, to exact nothing more — that is, to demand nothing *beyond what is due*; but to the soldiers, not to plunder, but to be content with their wages — that is, with the bounties customarily given by the king.[^1] But observe how to the crowds, as being guileless, he exhorts to do something good — that is, to share — whereas to the tax collectors and soldiers, to abstain from evil; for these were not yet capable *of it*, nor were they able to do anything good, but it was sufficient for them not to do evil.

But some also understand, according to the anagogical *sense*, the *saying* "him who has two tunics to share with him who has not"; for they say the two tunics are the spirit of Scripture and the letter. Him, then, who has the two, John urges to share with the one who is utterly naked; for instance: there is someone who understands Scripture in a twofold way, both according to the letter and according to the spirit — let him then share with the one who has not, let him teach the ignorant, and let him give him at least the letter.

So great was John's virtue that all reckoned concerning him, whether perhaps he himself is the Christ. Repelling, therefore, such a suspicion, he says, in effect: And so great is the difference between me and Christ, that I baptize in water, whereas he *is so great that*[^2] I am not worthy to loosen the strap of his sandals.

But what do these things signify? The *saying*, "He will baptize you with Spirit and fire," is altogether evident; for indeed he sent the Spirit to the apostles, and fiery tongues appeared, being distributed among them (Acts 2:3). But the *saying* that he is not worthy to loosen the strap of his sandals, according to the evident sense, *means* that: I am not worthy to be ranked even as his lowest slave. But according to the more hidden *sense*, there are two sandals[^3] of the Lord: the sojourn from heaven onto the earth, and the *descent* from earth to Hades; the manner, then, of these two sojournings no one is able to loosen,[^4] not even if he be like John. For who is able to say either how he was made flesh, or how he descended into Hades?

But the *saying* "Whose winnowing fork is in his hand" signifies this: that, even if he baptizes you, do not think that you are already guiltless; but unless you display a blameless life after these things, he will burn you up with unquenchable fire; for chaff is he who has a fruitless mind and carries about only a great heap of the rubbish of worldly affairs.

[^1]: Greek φιλοτιμίαις — largesses or gratuities bestowed out of liberality; here the customary allowances given to soldiers by the king.
[^2]: The OCR is defective here (ἐκεῖνος … ἱμάντα τῶν ὑποδημάτων αὐτοῦ ἱκανός εἰμι λῦσαι), evidently having lost the relative and negation (οὗ οὐδὲ τὸν ἱμάντα … οὐκ ἱκανός εἰμι λῦσαι); the reconstructed sense is supplied.
[^3]: OCR ἀποδήματα; read ὑποδήματα, "sandals" — the interpretation depends on the sandals of the preceding lemma.
[^4]: A wordplay on λῦσαι, which means both to "loosen/untie" the sandal-strap and to "unravel/explain" the mystery: the manner of the two comings cannot be untied — that is, cannot be explained — by anyone.
