# Luke 1 (Αʹ) — Lemma 43 · Luke 1:49

God has done great things with me. And she named him Mighty, so that no one might disbelieve the things said, reckoning that the Lord is not unable to do these things.[^1] And she called his name Holy, showing that the most pure one, being conceived in a woman's womb, is in no way defiled, but remains Holy. But his mercy is not upon me alone, but also upon all those who fear him; for those who do not fear him, but are altogether unworthy, are not shown mercy. And having said that the mercy of God is unto generation and generation, she showed this, that they are shown mercy both in the present generation, that is, in the present age, and in the generation to come, I mean the endless age; for both here they receive a hundredfold, and there much more.

But note that first the soul magnifies the Lord, then the spirit rejoices. I mean something like this: he who walks worthily of God magnifies God. You have been called a Christian; do not diminish the dignity of Christ and the name by doing the things that are not fitting, but magnify it, accomplishing great and heavenly deeds; and then your spirit also will rejoice, that is, the spiritual gift which you received through the great deeds will leap up and advance, but it will not be contracted and, so to speak, put to death.

And know that Scripture, speaking loosely, seems to call spirit and soul the same thing, but properly it distinguishes them; for it calls "soulish"[^2] the man living according to nature and governed by human reasonings — as when, being hungry,[^3] he eats, hating the enemy, and simply, imagining nothing above nature — but "spiritual" the man who has risen above the laws of nature and minds nothing human. And this is the difference between soul and spirit in Scripture. And perhaps physicians distinguish these otherwise; but we care about Scripture, and as for the physicians, let them perish.[^4]

[^1]: A wordplay: she names him "Mighty" (δυνατόν) precisely so that none should reckon the Lord "unable" (οὐκ ἀδύνατος) to accomplish these things.
[^2]: ψυχικόν — the adjective formed from ψυχή (soul); the "soulish" or natural man, as opposed to the "spiritual" (πνευματικόν). Rendered "soulish" to preserve the link to "soul."
[^3]: OCR corrupt here: the manuscript reads «τεινᾶ ἐσθίοντα», restored to πεινῶν (being hungry); rendered "being hungry, he eats."
[^4]: «ἰατροὶ δὲ φθειρέσθωσαν» — a dismissive idiom, literally "but let the physicians be destroyed / perish."
