# Luke 1 (Αʹ) — Lemma 29 · Luke 1:35

> **the angel answering said to her: The Holy Spirit shall come upon you, and the power of the Most High shall overshadow you, wherefore also the holy thing that is born shall be called Son of God. (Luke 1:35)**

Not as having disbelieved did the Virgin say the "How shall this be for me?" but as one wise and understanding, seeking to learn the manner of the matter; for nothing of the kind had happened before, nor will it happen afterward. Wherefore the angel pardons her, and does not condemn her as *he did* Zacharias, but rather interprets the manner. For Zacharias is reasonably condemned, since he had many examples that many barren women too had given birth; but the Virgin had no example.

A Holy Spirit, therefore, he says, shall come upon you, making your womb fruitful, and fashioning the flesh for the consubstantial Word; and a power of the Most High—the Son of God, for Christ is the power of God—shall overshadow you, that is, shall cover you over, shall encircle you on every side. For as a bird overshadows its own nestlings, embracing them all with its wings, so also the power of God embraced the whole Virgin; and this is what "to overshadow" means. And someone else perhaps will say that, just as the painter first sketches in shadow, then finishes the coloring,[^1] so also the Lord, himself fashioning the flesh for himself, and forming the image of the man, first shadowed it in the womb of the Virgin—having compacted flesh from the bloods of the Ever-Virgin—then shaped it little by little. But this is doubtful; for some say that at the same moment the Lord overshadowed the womb of the Virgin the infant was immediately complete, while others do not accept this. For hear what he says: Wherefore also the holy thing that is born—that is, the thing growing part by part in your womb, and not being immediately complete.

And from this too the mouth of Nestorius is stopped; for that one said that it was not the Son of God who, having dwelt in the womb of the Virgin, was made flesh, but a mere man born of Mary, who afterward had God accompanying him. Let him hear, therefore, that the thing born in the womb, that was Son of God; and it was not that one thing was carried in the womb and the Son of God another, but he was one and the same, the Son both of the Virgin and of God. And see how he revealed the Holy Trinity, naming the Holy Spirit, the Son as "power," and the Father as "Most High."

[^1]: The analogy turns on the shared root: ἐπισκιάσει ("shall overshadow") and the painter's σκιάζει ("shadows," i.e. lays in the preliminary underdrawing) echo one another; the same verb ἐσκίασε ("shadowed") is then applied to the Lord's forming of the flesh.
