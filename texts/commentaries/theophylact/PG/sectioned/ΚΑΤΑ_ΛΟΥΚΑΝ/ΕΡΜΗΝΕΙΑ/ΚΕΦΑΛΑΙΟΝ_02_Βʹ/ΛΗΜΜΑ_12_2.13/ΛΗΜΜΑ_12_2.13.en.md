# Luke 2 (Βʹ) — Lemma 12 · Luke 2:13

> **suddenly there was with the angel a multitude of the heavenly host, praising God and saying: Glory to God in the highest; and on earth peace, among men good will. (Luke 2:13)**

"Shepherds were lodging in the fields" (ἀγραυλοῦντες), that is, either piping a rustic strain, or bivouacking and passing the time in the field.[^1] And the angel appears to these, on account of the unaffectedness and guilelessness of their character. Since they also appear to imitate the manner of life of the righteous; for indeed the men of old, the shepherds, the patriarchs, were such: Jacob, Moses, David. For he did not appear in Jerusalem to Pharisees or Scribes—for those were receptacles of all wickedness—but these, being without guile, were deemed worthy of divine sights; God showing that he chose the more rustic of others from the very beginning, and made them heralds; for going away they proclaimed concerning all these things.

And the angel proclaimed good news of a great joy, which shall be, he says, to all the people—to the people that is properly God's; for not all the Jews are the people of God. But also to the whole people of men the incarnation of God became a joy. And what does the hymn of the angels signify? Assuredly the thanksgiving of the orders above, and joy, because we who are upon the earth have been benefited. "For glory to God," they say, "for peace on earth has now come to be"; for formerly human nature had enmity toward God, but now it has been so reconciled that it even entwined itself about God, and was united to him when he had been made flesh. Do you see, therefore, the peace of God toward man?

And it may also be understood otherwise: Peace is the Son of God himself; "For I," he says, "am the peace" (cf. Eph. 2:14); this peace, therefore—the Son of God—has come to be upon earth. "And among men good will"; that is, the repose of God; for God has now found repose and was well-pleased among men, who formerly indeed took no good pleasure, nor was pleased among men.[^2]

[^1]: Theophylact etymologizes ἀγραυλοῦντες, offering two derivations: from ἀγρόν + αὐλέω, "playing a rustic pipe" (ἀγροτικὸν αὐλοῦντες), or from ἀγρός + αὐλίζομαι, "lodging/passing the night in the field" (ἐν τῷ ἀγρῷ αὐλιζόμενοι). The word itself carries the second sense.
[^2]: On εὐδοκία ("good will") Theophylact plays throughout on the cognate verbs: he glosses it as ἐπανάπαυσις ("repose"), then says God «εὐηρεστήθη» ("was well-pleased") and formerly «οὐκ ηὐδόκει, οὐδὲ ἠρέσκετο» ("took no good pleasure, nor was pleased"). The single English "good pleasure / good will / well-pleased" cannot reproduce the εὐδοκ-/ἀρεσκ- interplay.
