# Luke 2 (Βʹ) — Lemma 33 · Luke 2:40

> **But the child grew and became strong in spirit, being filled with wisdom, and the grace of God was upon him. (Luke 2:40)**

The evangelist dwells upon the narrative concerning Anna, recounting both her father and her tribe, that we may learn that he tells the truth, calling forward as it were many witnesses, those who knew both the father and the tribe. She therefore returned thanks[^1] to the Lord, that is, she gave thanks, and she taught all men concerning the Lord, that he was the Savior and the consolation of us who await the redemption. But after the completing of all things, they returned into Galilee, into their own city Nazareth; for Bethlehem too was their city, but as a fatherland, whereas Nazareth was as a dwelling-place. And Jesus grew as to the body—although he was able,[^2] *even* from her womb, to rise up to the measure of a man's stature; but *had he done so* it would have seemed an illusion, and for this reason he grows little by little. And as he grew, the wisdom of God the Word was made manifest; for it was not by progress that he became wise—away with the thought! Rather, disclosing little by little the innate wisdom, in accordance with his bodily age he is said to advance and to grow strong in spirit. For if, while he was small in stature, he had disclosed all his wisdom, it would have seemed a portent; but as it is, showing himself proportionately to his age as far as was allowed, he was fulfilling the economy, not anticipating wisdom (for what could become more perfect than that which is perfect from the beginning?), but the …[^3]

[^1]: Greek «ἀνθωμολογεῖτο», which Theophylact himself glosses with «ηὐχαρίστει» ("she gave thanks"). The compound ἀνθομολογέομαι carries the sense of open acknowledgment or grateful confession made in return; hence "returned thanks," distinguished here from the plainer "gave thanks" of the gloss.

[^2]: OCR: the Greek reads «δενάμενος»; read «δυνάμενος» ("being able").

[^3]: The comment breaks off here mid-sentence at the fragment boundary; the Greek ends «ἀλλὰ τὴν» ("but the …").
