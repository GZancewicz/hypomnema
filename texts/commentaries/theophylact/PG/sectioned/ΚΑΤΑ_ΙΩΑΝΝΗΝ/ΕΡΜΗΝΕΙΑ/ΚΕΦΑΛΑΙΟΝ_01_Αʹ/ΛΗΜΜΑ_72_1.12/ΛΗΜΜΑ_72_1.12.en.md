# John 1 (Αʹ) — Lemma 72 · John 1:12

> **"Nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man." (John 1:12)**

For indeed, even if Isaac's birth was not of bloods, yet it was of the will of man; for the man surely willed that a child be born to him from Sarah. But of the will of the flesh, as Samuel from Hannah. So you might say that Isaac *was* of the will of man, but Samuel of the will of the flesh—that is, of the human *nature*;[^1] for this barren woman thirsted to obtain a child. But perhaps both *apply* to both.

But if you should wish to learn something else too, hear: that fleshly intercourse comes either from natural inflammation—for often someone has happened to have a hotter constitution, and from this is prone to intercourse (this, then, he named "the will of the flesh")—or, through evil habit and unbridled living, the impulse to intercourse becomes uncontrollable, which he called "the will of man," as being the work not of the natural constitution but of the man's licentiousness.

But perhaps, since sometimes the woman is found prone to intercourse and sometimes the man, by "the will of man" he indicated the lust of the man, and by "the will of the flesh" that of the woman. And you might reasonably understand also "the will of the flesh" *as* the desire that rekindles the flesh toward union, and "the will of man" *as* the consent of the one desiring toward the coupling, which is the beginning of the act. And he set down both, because many desire, yet do not straightway get carried off along with the desire, but master it and do not slip toward the act. But as many as are overcome, those, being first inflamed by the flesh and by the desire smouldering within it, turn aside toward the willing to be united. In good order, therefore, the evangelist placed "the will of the flesh" before "the will of man," since the desire naturally precedes the union, and both necessarily run together at the coupling.

But all these things have been said on account of those who often ask foolish questions; since, to speak of the more principal point, one thought is indicated through all these—I mean, the emphasis on the cheapness of the fleshly birth. What more, then, do we who believe in Christ have than the Israelites under the law? For they too were called sons of God. Great is the difference between us and them. [^1]: The Greek has a lacuna after τῆς ἀνθρωπίνης ("of the human..."); the noun (σαρκός, "flesh," or φύσεως, "nature") has dropped out in the source—the OCR shows a stray "(·" at this point. "nature" is supplied by conjecture.
[^2]: OCR reads "Ἀδίου," which is corrupt; the sense requires a verb such as ἐδίδου ("granted/gave"), here reconstructed: οὐδὲ τὴν υἱοθεσίαν ἔχειν ἐδίδου τοῖς Ἰσραηλίταις.
[^3]: The sentence runs on into the next section, which supplies the cry—"Abba, Father" (cf. Rom. 8:15). OCR of this section reads several forms corruptly, rendered here from the evident text: ἔτυχε, θερμοτέρας, ὠνόμασε, ἔννοια (for "Πννοια"), τόκου (for "τόπου"), ἔμφασις (for "ἱμφασις").
