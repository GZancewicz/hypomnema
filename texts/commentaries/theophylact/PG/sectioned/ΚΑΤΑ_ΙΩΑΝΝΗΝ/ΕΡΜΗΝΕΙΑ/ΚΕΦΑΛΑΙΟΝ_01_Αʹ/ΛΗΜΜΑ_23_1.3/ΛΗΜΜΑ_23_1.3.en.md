# John 1 (Αʹ) — Lemma 23 · John 1:3

> **And the Word was God. (John 1:1)**

— so that no satanic suspicion might throw certain people into confusion, namely: Might it perhaps be that, since the Word too is God, he has risen up in revolt against the Father, like the beings fabled among the Greeks, and, as it were, having separated himself from him, has become a rival god [ἀντίθεος]? For this reason, therefore, he says that, even if the Word is God, yet nevertheless he is again with God and the Father, being with him and never being separated.

And it is not amiss to say this also against those who Arianize: You deaf ones, hear — you who say that the Son of God is a work and a thing made of God: understand what name the evangelist has bestowed upon the Son of God. For he called him Word; but you name him a work and a thing made. He is Word, not work, nor creature.

Now the word is twofold. For one is the inward word [ἐνδιάθετος], which we have even when not speaking — I mean the faculty of speaking; for even one who is asleep, and not speaking, nonetheless has the word lying within him, and has not cast off the faculty. The one, then, is inward, and the other is uttered [προφορικός], which we bring forth through the lips, leading into activity the faculty of speaking that belongs to the inward word lying within. Since, then, the word is twofold, neither of these fits in the case of the Son of God. For the Word of God is neither uttered nor inward. For those belong to natural things and to what is according to us; but the Word of the Father, being above nature, is not subjected to the lower technical distinctions.

So that the sophism of the Greek Porphyry has collapsed. For he, attempting to overturn the Gospel, made use of such divisions. For if the Son of God is Word, he says, he is either uttered or inward; [but he is neither of these].[^1] The evangelist, then, has already resolved this sophism beforehand, by saying that the inward and the uttered are spoken of in the case of what is according to us and of natural things, but in the case of what is above nature there is nothing of the sort.

Nevertheless this too must be said: that if this name "Word" were worthy of God, and were said of him properly and essentially, the Greek's difficulty would have some reason. But as it is, one could neither find any other name that is proper in the case of God, nor is the term "Word" itself [used] essentially and properly; rather, it is only indicative of the Son's being begotten impassibly from the Father — just as the word [proceeds] from the mind — and of his having become the messenger [ἄγγελος] of the Father's will. So why do you fasten upon the name, O wretched one? Surely, when you hear also "Father" and "Son" and "Spirit," you will not fall down to material things and picture in your mind fleshly fathers and sons, and an airy spirit — a south wind, perhaps, or a north wind, or some other of the things that work up the surging waves? But indeed, if you wish to learn what sort of Word the Word of God is, hear what follows.

[^1]: The Greek here has a lacuna: the manuscript runs «ἤτοι προφορικός ἐστιν ἢ ἐνδιάθετος· … ἐστίν», with the minor premise dropped and only a stray «ἐστίν» surviving. The bracketed clause supplies the sense demanded by the argument (Porphyry's dilemma: the Word must be either uttered or inward — but it is neither), which Theophylact goes on to refute in the next sentence.
