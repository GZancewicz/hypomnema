# Mark 8 (Ηʹ) — Lemma 36 · Mark 8:37

> **shall a man give in exchange for his soul? (Mark 8:37)**

Since Peter had rebuked him as one wishing to be crucified, he calls the crowd to himself, and, in the hearing of all, directing his words the more toward Peter, he says: "You blame me, O Peter, because I choose the cross? But I say to you, that neither you nor anyone else will be saved, unless he die for the good and for the truth." And observe that he did not say, "Even if one does not wish, let him die," but, "Whoever wishes." "For I compel no one," he says. "For I call to good things, not to evil things, that I should also constrain; so that if anyone does not wish, he is not even worthy of these things." But what it is to deny oneself, we would learn thus, if we know what it is to deny another. He who denies another—a brother perhaps, or a household servant, or a father—even if he should see him being scourged, even if being murdered, does not turn to him, does not suffer with him, inasmuch as he has once for all been estranged from him. So therefore he wishes us also to be unsparing of our own body, so that even if they scourge us, even if they do anything whatever, we should not spare it. "And let him take up his cross," that is, the shameful death; for the cross seemed shameful then. But since many are crucified for being robbers, he adds that, together with being crucified, one must have also the other virtue. For this is the meaning of "And let him follow me." But since this which he was commanding seemed to be burdensome and harsh—to give oneself over to death—he says that, on the contrary, this is rather a thing of love toward man. For whoever shall lose his life[^1]—but for my sake, not as a robber being murdered, or hanging himself (for this is not "for my sake")—such a one, then, will save his own life; so that, again, he who seems to gain it will lose it, namely, whoever at the time of martyrdom has not stood firm.[^2] For do not tell me, "But he gained his life." For if along with this you should further suppose that he gained also the whole world, there is no profit. For is it possible to buy back salvation with money? For if this were so, he who had gained the world but lost his soul would then give it—while being fried in the flame—and be acquitted. But there is no such exchange there, then. And from this, therefore, we may also silence those of Origen's following, who say that there will be a restoration of souls after they have been punished according to the proportion of their sins. For let them hear that it is not possible there to give an exchange for the soul. Accordingly, neither is it possible to be punished only so far as to be counterbalanced against one's sins[^3] and thereafter released.

[^1]: ψυχή is rendered here both "life" and "soul." In "lose his life... save his own life" the sense is one's physical life laid down for Christ (8:35); in "lost his soul" and "an exchange for the soul" the sense is the eternal soul.
[^2]: μὴ ἀντιστάς: literally "not having stood against, not having withstood"—i.e., the one who, at the moment of martyrdom, did not stand firm but yielded (denying Christ to save his life).
[^3]: ἐντισηκωθῆναι (from ἀντισηκόω, "to counterbalance, offset"): the point against Origen's ἀποκατάστασις is that punishment cannot be merely proportional so as to balance the account and end. OCR reads «ἐντισηκωθῆναι».
