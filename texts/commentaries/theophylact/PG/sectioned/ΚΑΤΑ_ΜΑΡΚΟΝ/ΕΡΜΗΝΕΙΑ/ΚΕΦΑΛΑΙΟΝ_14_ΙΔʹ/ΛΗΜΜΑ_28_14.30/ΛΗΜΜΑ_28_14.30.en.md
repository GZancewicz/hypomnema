# Mark 14 (ΙΔʹ) — Lemma 28 · Mark 14:30

> **Jesus says to him: Amen I say to you, that you today, on this night, before the cock crows twice, will deny me three times. But he was saying all the more vehemently: Even if I must die together with you, I will by no means deny you. And likewise all were saying the same. (Mark 14:30-31)**

They gave thanks both before drinking, and they gave thanks also after drinking, so that we too may learn that one must give thanks and sing hymns both before the meal and after the meal. And at the same time he shows this too, that the death on our behalf is welcome to him, seeing that, going out to be handed over, he sings a hymn to God. And in every way he also teaches us, whenever we fall into afflictions for the salvation of many, not to be despondent, but to give thanks to God, who through our affliction works out the salvation of many.

But he goes out to the Mount of Olives, so that those coming upon him and seizing him while he was alone might not make so great an uproar. For if they had come upon him while he was in the city, perhaps the crowd would have been stirred up over him; and then the enemies, having seized upon a plausible pretext,[^1] would have seemed to kill him justly as an insurrectionist. And he prophesies to them that they too will be made to stumble. Then, so that such a thing might not seem to be a manifest accusation of all, he brings forward also a testimony from the prophet Zechariah (Zech. 13:7), that they will be scattered. And lastly he brings upon them a consolation as well, that "I will go before you into Galilee," that is, "I will get there ahead of you." But Peter sets himself against this, and therefore he also hears that "Before the cock crows twice, you will deny me three times."

And this is of such a kind: Peter denied once; then a cock crowed; and when he had made the other two denials as well, then the cock crowed. This, therefore, is what he says here: "Before the cock crows twice, you will deny me three times." And likewise all the rest too, displaying a cold fervor,[^2] kept making promises—making the Truth-itself to lie;[^3] wherefore the Lord also yields, and human nature displays what is its own. For the Lord was altogether able to preserve them, and especially Peter; but nevertheless he let them go, so that we might not be trusting in ourselves.

But the phrase "I will strike the shepherd" the Father says. For since he permitted him to be struck, he himself is said to strike the one struck according to his permission. And he named the apostles "sheep," as being guileless.[^4]

[^1]: εὐλογοφανοῦς (OCR «τὐλογοφανοῦς»), "having a plausible / specious appearance."
[^2]: θερμότητα ψυχρὰν, lit. "a cold heat/fervor"—a deliberate oxymoron: their zeal was hollow and would soon grow cold.
[^3]: τὴν αὐτοαλήθειαν ψεύδεσθαι ποιοῦντες, "making the Truth-itself to lie"—i.e., by promising what they would not do, they were in effect making Christ, who is Truth itself, out to be a liar.
[^4]: ἀκάκους, "guileless / without malice." A few garbled OCR characters (an unreadable marginal note) follow ἀκά(κους) in the source and carry no legible text.
