# Matthew 21 (ΚΑʹ) — Lemma 28 · Matt. 21:26–28

> **—we say, From men, we fear the crowd; for all hold John as a prophet. And answering Jesus they said: We do not know. And he too said to them: Neither do I tell you by what authority I do these things. (Matt. 21:26–27)**

The teachers of the law, envying *him* because he had cast out from the temple those who trafficked *there*, come forward asking him some such things as *these*: Who *are* you *who* cast out from the temple those doing business? Then, *is it* as a priest? and yet you do not have priestly office. Then, therefore, as a king? and yet neither are you a king, nor, if you were, would it be permitted you to do such things; for it is not permitted to kings to do these things in *the* temple. But they asked these things of the Lord in order that — if on the one hand he should say, "By my own authority I do these things," they might slander him as a rebel[^1] and as one saying that he has his own authority; but if on the other hand he should say, "By the divine authority I do these things," they might draw the crowds away from him — the *crowds* that were praising him as God — having shown to the crowds that, "Behold, he himself is not God, but by the authority of God he does these things as a servant."

What then *does* Christ, the very Wisdom,[^2] *do*? He seizes the wise in their own malice,[^3] and asks them the like concerning John, so that — if on the one hand they should say that the preaching of John was from heaven, they might be found fighters against God,[^4] as not having accepted this; but if on the other hand they should say "from men," they might be endangered by the crowd. For all held John as a prophet. And the Lord shows from this that to those who ask with malicious intent one ought not to answer. For neither did he himself answer the Jews who were asking him with guile, although he was not at a loss for an answer. And at the same time we learn that to praise oneself, too, is not according to Christ. Behold, therefore, the Lord, though able to say by what authority he does these things, did not say *it*, lest he should seem to praise himself.

> **But what does it seem to you?[^5] A man had two children; and having come to the first, he said: Child, go today, work in my vineyard. But he—[^6]**

[^1]: Greek «ἀντάρτην» — a rebel, insurgent, one who rises up *against* established authority.
[^2]: Greek «ἡ αὐτοσοφία» — "Wisdom-itself, the very Wisdom," a title for Christ (cf. 1 Cor. 1:24, 30).
[^3]: An allusion to Job 5:13, quoted at 1 Cor. 3:19: «ὁ δρασσόμενος τοὺς σοφοὺς ἐν τῇ πανουργίᾳ αὐτῶν» ("who catches the wise in their craftiness"). Theophylact here writes «κακουργίᾳ» ("malice, villainy, wrongdoing") in place of the scriptural «πανουργίᾳ» ("cunning, craftiness"); rendered "malice" to reflect his word.
[^4]: Greek «θεομάχοι» — "God-fighters," men found fighting against God (cf. Acts 5:39).
[^5]: Greek «Τί δὲ ὑμῖν δοκεῖ;» — literally "But what seems to you?", i.e. "But what do you think?"
[^6]: The scripture resumes with the opening of the parable of the two sons (Matt. 21:28) and breaks off at «Ὁ δὲ» ("But he—"), completed in the next lemma.
