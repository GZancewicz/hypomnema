# Matthew 16 (ΙϚʹ) — Lemma 22 · Matt. 16:23[^1]

> **But he, having turned, said to Peter: Get behind me, Satan; you are a scandal[^2] to me, because you do not mind the things of God, but the things of men. (Matt. 16:23)**

When, on the one hand, Peter was speaking right *things*, Christ blesses him; but when, on the other hand, he was irrationally cowardly and did not wish him to suffer, he rebuked *him*, saying: "Get behind me, Satan." And "Satan" means "the adversary."[^3] "Get behind me," therefore — that is, "do not set yourself against *me*, but follow my will." And he calls Peter thus, since to Satan too the suffering of Christ was a thing unwished-for. He says, therefore: "You, on the one hand, by human reasoning think the passion to be unbecoming for me; but you do not perceive that God through this works salvation, and that this befits me most of all."

[^1]: The lemma folder is labelled 16.22, but the Greek quoted here (*Ὕπαγε ὀπίσω μου, Σατανᾶ …*) is Matt. 16:23; the header and reference are corrected accordingly.
[^2]: Greek *σκάνδαλόν μου εἶ* — "you are a *σκάνδαλον* (stumbling-block / snare / cause of falling) to me." Rendered "scandal" to keep the σκάνδαλον word-group visible across the surrounding lemmata; "stumbling-block" is the plainer sense.
[^3]: Greek *Σατανᾶς … ὁ ἀντικείμενος λέγεται* — Theophylact glosses the Hebrew name by its meaning, "the one who sets himself against, the opponent / adversary." The same verb (*ἀνθίστασο*, "set yourself against") recurs in the next sentence, tying the gloss to the command.
