# Matthew 10 (Ιʹ) — Lemma 5 · Matt. 10:3–4

> **Philip and Bartholomew, Thomas and Matthew the tax-collector, James the son of Alphaeus, and Lebbaeus who was surnamed Thaddaeus. Simon the Cananite; and Judas Iscariot, who also betrayed him.[^1] (Matt. 10:3–4)**

Behold the humility of Matthew, how he ranked himself after Thomas.[^2] And coming to Judas, the abominable *one*, the enemy of God, he did not say *so*, but named him "Iscariot" from his native place.[^3] For there was also another Judas, the one *called* both Lebbaeus and Thaddaeus. Two, accordingly, *were the* Jameses — the *son* of Zebedee and the *son* of Alphaeus — and two Judases: Thaddaeus and the betrayer. And three Simons: Peter, the Cananite, and the betrayer. For Judas Iscariot was called *son of* Simon[^4]

[^1]: OCR reads «Σίκων» for «Σίμων» and «ατθαῖος» for «Ματθαῖος»; both securely recoverable. *Κανανίτης*, "Cananite" (from Aramaic *qanʾan*, "zealot"; cf. Luke 6:15 "the Zealot").
[^2]: The point turns on the ordering: in the parallel lists of Mark 3:18 and Luke 6:15 Matthew stands before Thomas, whereas here the evangelist, writing of himself, places himself second — and adds "the tax-collector." Greek *ταπείνωσις*, "humility, lowliness."
[^3]: Greek «οὐκ εἶπεν ὁ μιαρός, ὁ τοῦ Θεοῦ ἐχθρός, ἀλλ᾽ Ἰσκαριώτην αὐτὸν ἀπὸ τῆς πατρίδος ὠνόμασεν» — literally "he did not say 'the abominable one, the enemy of God,' but named him Iscariot from *his* homeland." *πατρίς* = "native town, fatherland"; "Iscariot" is understood as *ish-Kerioth*, "man of Kerioth." *μιαρός* = "defiled, abominable, foul."
[^4]: The Greek breaks off here: «Σίμων γὰρ Ἰούδας Ἰσκαριώτης ἐλέγετο» — literally "For Simon Judas Iscariot was called," which is elliptical. The reckoning of "three Simons" makes the betrayer a third bearer of the name only through his father: in John 6:71 Judas is «Ἰούδας Σίμωνος Ἰσκαριώτης», "Judas *son of* Simon Iscariot." Hence the supplied "*son of*." The clause is left incomplete in the source and is not reconstructed further.
