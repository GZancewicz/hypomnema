# Matthew 10 (Ιʹ) — Lemma 30 · Matt. 10:28

> **And do not fear *those* who kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul; but fear rather *him* who is able to destroy both soul and body in Gehenna.**[^1]

He teaches *them* also to despise death; for more fearful, he says, is the punishment in Gehenna. For those who kill, on the one hand, work destruction of the flesh only, but the soul perhaps they even benefit; but God, on the other hand, casting *a man* into Gehenna, punishes both — both soul and body.[^2] And in saying "in Gehenna," he signifies the perpetuity of the punishment; for "Gehenna" *is derived* from "for ever."[^3]

[^1]: Greek «Καὶ μὴ φοβηθῆτε ἀπὸ τῶν ἀποκτεινόντων τὸ σῶμα, τὴν δὲ ψυχὴν μὴ δυναμένων ἀποκτεῖναι, φοβήθητε δὲ μᾶλλον τὸν δυνάμενον καὶ ψυχὴν καὶ σῶμα ἀπολέσαι ἐν γεέννῃ» — Matt. 10:28. The construction *φοβηθῆτε ἀπό* ("fear *away* from," a Hebraism carried into the Greek) is rendered plainly "fear *those* who…"; *ἀπολέσαι* is "destroy / ruin / lose," here of both soul and body.
[^2]: OCR *ρεὸς* is securely *Θεός* ("God"). The reconstruction is certain from the sense and the contrast with the human *οἱ θανατοῦντες* ("those who kill").
[^3]: Theophylact gives a folk etymology: *Γέεννα γὰρ, παρὰ τὸ ἀεί* — "for *Gehenna* is *derived* from *ἀεί* ('always, for ever')," making the very name signify the endlessness (*τὸ διηνεκές*) of the punishment. This is a Byzantine etymological word-play, not a historical derivation (the name is in fact from Hebrew *gê-hinnōm*, "Valley of Hinnom"); it is footnoted because the point turns entirely on the Greek *ἀεί*, which cannot be shown in the English "for ever."
