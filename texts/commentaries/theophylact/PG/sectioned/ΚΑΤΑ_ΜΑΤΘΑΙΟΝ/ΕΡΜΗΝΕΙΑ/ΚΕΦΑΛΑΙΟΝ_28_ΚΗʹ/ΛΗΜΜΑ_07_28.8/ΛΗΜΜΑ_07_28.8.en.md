# Matthew 28 (ΚΗʹ) — Lemma 7 · Matt. 28:5-8

> **Take, eat my body.**[^1]

So then, since he had authority of himself to lay down his life,[^2] *it is* clear that from that time he slaughtered[^3] himself, from *the moment* when he was distributing his body to his own disciples; for no one eats anything, unless it has first been slaughtered. Count, therefore: in the evening he was distributing his own body; that night, and the day of the Preparation[^4] until the sixth hour — behold, one day-and-night;[^5] again, the darkness from the sixth hour until the ninth, and the light from the ninth until evening — behold, a second day-and-night; again, the night after the Preparation, and the day of the Sabbath — behold, a third day-and-night. And late on the Sabbath he rose; these *are* three complete days-and-nights.

But *as for* the angel — Matthew, on the one hand, said that he sat upon the stone; but Mark, on the other hand, *said* that, having rolled away the stone, he sat within the tomb, on the right. Do they then contradict *one another*?[^6] No; but it is likely that the angel appeared first sitting upon the stone, then, when the women had entered, that the angel went before *them*, and appeared again within the tomb, sitting on the right. And he said to the women: Fear not ye — that is, The guards, on the one hand, are worthy to fear; but ye, the disciples[^7] of the Lord, fear not. And after he releases them from *their* dread, he announces to them the good news of the resurrection. For it was necessary first to cast out the dread, and then to announce the good news. And he is not ashamed to name the Lord *as* crucified. For in the cross, as in some trophy, he glories, since it has procured for us all good things[^8]

[^1]: The lemma is Matt. 26:26, the Last Supper saying. The common text reads «Λάβετε, φάγετε· τοῦτό ἐστι τὸ σῶμά μου» ("Take, eat; this is my body"); Theophylact abbreviates to «Λάβετε, φάγετε τὸ σῶμά μου» ("Take, eat my body"). It completes the sentence broken off at the end of the previous lemma ("and to the disciples he said:") and is cited here within the argument counting the three days and three nights — not as a fresh resurrection-narrative lemma. The header verse follows what this lemma actually expounds (the angel and his words, Matt. 28:5-8), rather than the embedded quotation.
[^2]: Greek *θεῖναι τὴν ψυχὴν αὐτοῦ*, the fixed Johannine idiom "to lay down one's life" (John 10:18, *ἐξουσίαν ἔχω θεῖναι αὐτήν*, "I have authority to lay it down"). *ψυχή* can mean "soul," but in this idiom the primary sense is "life," which is followed here.
[^3]: Greek *ἀπέσφαξεν ἑαυτόν*, from *ἀποσφάζω* ("slaughter, slay *as a sacrifice*"). The strong, sacrificial verb is retained deliberately — the point is that in distributing his body he had already, as it were, offered himself as victim.
[^4]: Greek *ἡ Παρασκευή*, "the Preparation" (Friday), the day before the Sabbath. Kept as the proper name rather than "Friday."
[^5]: Greek *ἡμερονύκτιον* (nychthemeron), a single "day-and-night," i.e. a full 24-hour span reckoned as one unit. The whole passage turns on counting three such units.
[^6]: The OCR reads *ἐναντιοκωνοῦσιν*, garbled for *ἐναντιοῦνται* ("are they opposed / do they contradict"), or possibly *ἐναντία φωνοῦσιν* ("do they speak contrary things"). The sense is secure.
[^7]: Greek *μαθήτριαι*, the feminine of "disciples" — "the *women*-disciples of the Lord." English "disciples" loses the feminine marking, which is explicit here.
[^8]: The lemma breaks off with the participial clause *πάντα τὰ ἀγαθὰ προεξενηκότι ἡμῖν* ("*the cross,* which has procured for us all good things beforehand"), agreeing with "cross"; the thought is essentially complete but trails at the section division.
