# Matthew 25 (ΚΕʹ) — Lemma 39 · Matt. 25:46

> **—[whence] these shall go away into eternal punishment and never ceasing;[^1] but the righteous into eternal life. (Matt. 25:46)**

For just as the saints have unceasing joy, so also the unrighteous *have* unceasing punishment, even though Origen talks nonsense,[^2] saying that there is an end of punishment, and that sinners will not be punished forever, but that there will be *a time* when they will come into the place of the righteous, having been purified through the punishment. For he is refuted plainly from this — on the one hand, in the words in which the Lord says "eternal punishment," that is, never ceasing; and on the other hand, in *the words* in which he likens the righteous to sheep, but the sinners to goats. For just as a goat could never become a sheep, so neither will the sinner ever be purified and become righteous.

But the "outer" darkness[^3] *is* that which is farther from the divine light, and for this reason *it* also makes the punishment more grievous. And it is possible also to state the following reason for this: the sinner, on the one hand, even here has darkness, having fallen away from the Sun of righteousness; but since there is hope of change, for this reason this darkness is not "outer." After he dies, however, and the examination of the things *he has* done comes upon *him*, the "outer" *darkness* also succeeds to receive him; for there is no longer hope of change, but he undergoes a total deprivation of the good things of God. For as long as he is here, even if he enjoys the good things of God only moderately — I mean the perceptible things of creation — and seems in some way to be a servant of God, living in his house, this creation, and being nourished and sustained by him; then, however, he is utterly cut off from God, partaking of none of his good things. This is the darkness called, comparatively, "outer," in relation to the *darkness* here — which is not "outer," inasmuch as the sinner is not yet, already from here, utterly cut off.

But you, flee heartlessness, and work mercy — on the one hand perceptibly, but nonetheless also spiritually.[^4] For feed Christ, who hungers for our salvation; but also, if you feed and give drink to him who hungers for teaching, you have fed Christ and given *him* drink. For the Christ in the Christian, and the faith, is nourished and grows through teaching. But even if you see someone becoming a stranger to the homeland above, bring *him* in together *with you* — that is, along with your own entering into the heavens, bring that man in also, not preaching to others while you yourself are disqualified.[^5] And if anyone casts off the garment of incorruption, which he received through baptism, and is stripped naked, clothe him; and him who is weak in the faith, as Paul says, receive *to yourself*;[^6] and him who is shut up in the prison — this dark body — visit, bestowing upon him admonition like some light. All these *works* of love[^7] accomplish both bodily and, nonetheless, also with respect to the soul; for since we are twofold, of soul and body, these things can be accomplished in a twofold manner.

[^1]: The lemma resumes and completes «ὅθεν ἀπελεύσονται οὗτοι εἰς κόλασιν αἰώνιον» from the end of Lemma 38 (the supplied "[whence]" marks that continuation). The words «καὶ μηδέποτε λήγουσαν» ("and never ceasing") are Theophylact's own expansion woven into the Gospel citation, not part of Matt. 25:46; they anticipate the point he presses against Origen. Retained in roman as they stand in his Greek.
[^2]: Greek *κἂν Ὠριγένης φλυαρῇ* ("even if Origen talks nonsense / babbles"). The reference is to the doctrine of *ἀποκατάστασις* (universal restoration), which Theophylact rejects. OCR «Ὡριγένης» corrected to Ὠριγένης.
[^3]: *σκότος ἐξώτερον*, "the outer darkness" (cf. Matt. 8:12; 22:13; 25:30). Theophylact's whole argument turns on the comparative force of *ἐξώτερον* ("outer / further out"): the present darkness of the sinner is not yet "outer" because change is still possible, whereas after death the total severance from God is the darkness properly called "outer." The scare-quotes on "outer" throughout preserve his technical use of the term.
[^4]: A recurring antithesis in this passage: *αἰσθητῶς* ("perceptibly / with the senses / bodily") vs. *πνευματικῶς* / *ψυχικῶς* ("spiritually / with respect to the soul"). Theophylact reads each corporal work of mercy (feed, give drink, take in, clothe, visit) as having both a bodily and a spiritual fulfillment.
[^5]: Greek *ἀδόκιμος ὤν*, alluding to 1 Cor. 9:27 («lest… I myself should be disqualified [ἀδόκιμος]»). *συνεισάγαγε* ("bring in together with") is rendered with supplied italic "*him*" and "*with you*."
[^6]: An allusion to Rom. 14:1: «τὸν ἀσθενοῦντα τῇ πίστει προσλαμβάνεσθε» ("him that is weak in the faith receive"). *προσλαβοῦ* rendered "receive *to yourself*."
[^7]: The source is OCR-corrupt here: «Ταῦτα πάντα τὰ ἐξ ειαη τῆς ἀγάπης». The words «ἐξ ειαη» are garbled and not securely recoverable; the sense is clearly "all these [works/things] of love." Rendered "All these *works* of love," with the supplied noun in italics and this note flagging the corruption; a plausible underlying text is «τὰ ἐξ [ἀγάπης] τῆς ἀγάπης» or a phrase such as «τὰ ἐξ εἰλικρινοῦς… ἀγάπης», but this cannot be confirmed.
