# Matthew 11 (ΙΑʹ) — Lemma 1 · Matt. 11:29-30[^1]

> **Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me that I am meek and lowly in heart, and you shall find rest for your souls. For my yoke is good,[^2] and my burden is light. (Matt. 11:29-30)**

The yoke of Christ is humility and meekness. He, at any rate, who humbles himself to every man has rest, remaining untroubled — just as the vainglorious and the boaster is always in anxieties, not wishing to be made less than anyone, but reckoning how he shall be more glorified, how he shall conquer his enemies. The yoke, accordingly, of Christ — the humility, I mean — is light; for it is easier for our lowly nature to be humbled than to be exalted. But also all the commandments of Christ are called a yoke, which are light on account of the coming recompense, even if for a time they seem heavy.

[^1]: This lemma stands at the head of the chapter-12 section in the source, but the Gospel words it expounds are the close of chapter 11 (Matt. 11:29-30, the "yoke" saying) — a transitional recapitulation before the Sabbath-controversy narrative that opens chapter 12. The header verse is corrected accordingly; the folder metadata (12.1) is retained only as the section locator.
[^2]: Greek *χρηστός* — "good, kind, benevolent, serviceable"; of a yoke, "easy to bear." Rendered "good" to keep the plain adjective; the sense shades toward "kindly / easy," and Theophylact's own gloss below turns rather on *ἐλαφρός* ("light"). The clause is verbless (*Ὁ γὰρ ζυγός μου χρηστός*); the supplied copula "is" is left roman.
[^3]: The Greek here lists the chapter headings (κεφάλαια / titloi) of the pericopae that follow: «Περὶ τῶν μαθητῶν τοῖς Σάββασι τιλλόντων στάχυας. Περὶ τοῦ ξηρὰν ἔχοντος τὴν χεῖρα. Περὶ τοῦ δαιμονιζομένου τυφλοῦ καὶ κωφοῦ. Περὶ τῶν αἰτούντων σημεῖον. Περὶ Μητρὸς καὶ ἀδελφῶν τοῦ Χριστοῦ.» (OCR «κίλοοῦ» → *κωφοῦ* "mute/deaf"; «ἀθελφῶν» → *ἀδελφῶν* "brethren"). These are editorial rubrics, not Theophylact's exposition.
