# Matthew 9 (Θʹ) — Lemma 13 · Matt. 9:12–13

> **But Jesus, having heard, said to them: "Those who are strong have no need of a physician, but those who are ill.[^1] But having gone, learn what this is: 'I desire mercy, and not sacrifice.'[^2] For I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance." (Matt. 9:12–13)**

I came not, he says, now as a judge, but as a physician; whence also I endure the stench.[^3] And he reproves them also as unlearned, saying, "Having gone, learn" — that is, since up to the present you have not learned, at least from now go away and learn, that God prefers mercy toward sinners to the sacrifices. But the *phrase*, "I came not to call the righteous," he says with irony[^4] — that is, you who justify yourselves — since no one of men is righteous. I came therefore to call sinners, not that they might remain sinners, but that they might repent.

[^1]: Greek *οἱ κακῶς ἔχοντες*, literally "those who are faring badly," i.e. the sick — set against *οἱ ἰσχύοντες*, "those who are strong / in health."
[^2]: The lemma quotes «Ἔλεον θέλω, καὶ οὐ θυσίαν», a citation of Hos. 6:6 ("I desire mercy and not sacrifice"). The masculine/neuter *ἔλεον* stands for the more usual neuter *ἔλεος*; rendered "mercy." Translated from Theophylact's own wording rather than any English Bible.
[^3]: Greek *ὅθεν καὶ δυσωδίας ἀνέχομαι*, "whence also I bear/endure *the* foul smell" — extending the physician image: as the doctor tolerates the stench of wounds, so Christ tolerates the company of sinners.
[^4]: Greek *εἰρωνευόμενος λέγει*, "he speaks with irony (dissemblingly)"; the "righteous" whom he did not come to call are named ironically — namely the Pharisees who reckon themselves righteous.
