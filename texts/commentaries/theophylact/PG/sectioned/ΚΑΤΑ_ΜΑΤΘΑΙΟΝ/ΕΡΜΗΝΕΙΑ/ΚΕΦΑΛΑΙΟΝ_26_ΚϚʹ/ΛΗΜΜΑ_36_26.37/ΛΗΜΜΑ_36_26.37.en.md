# Matthew 26 (ΚϚʹ) — Lemma 36 · Matt. 26:37–39

> **And having taken along Peter and the two sons of Zebedee, he began to be grieved and to be in anguish;[^1] then he says to them: My soul is exceedingly sorrowful, unto death; remain here and watch with me. And having gone forward a little, he fell upon his face, praying and saying: My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from me. Nevertheless, not as I will, but as you *will*. (Matt. 26:37–39)**[^2]

He does not take along all the disciples, but the three to whom he showed the glory on Tabor, in order that, having seen him praying and in anguish, they might not be offended; yet nevertheless he leaves even these, and, having gone away, he prays quite alone. And by *divine* dispensation[^3] he is grieved and in anguish, so that he might give assurance that he was truly a man. For to fear death belongs to human nature; because death also entered contrary to nature, and for this reason nature flees it.[^4] And at the same time, *it was* also in order that he might escape the notice of the devil, and that *the devil* might leap upon him as upon a mere man,[^5] so as to put him to death, and thus be crushed. Besides, if the Lord had leapt upon death, he would have given the Jews a defense, as *though* they had not sinned in killing one who leapt upon the passion.[^6] And from this we too learn not to hurl ourselves into temptations, but to pray to be delivered from them; for on account of this he also does not go off a great distance, but *stays* near the three disciples, in order that, hearing him, they too, when they have fallen into temptations, might remember and pray the like.

And he names the passion "a cup":[^7] either because of the sleep *that comes* from it; or because it is a cause of gladness and salvific; or in order that he might show that, as a man, by natural laws he begs off from death, as has been said before; or because he did not wish the Hebrews to commit so great a sin, such that on account of it there should come the pulling-down of the temple and the ruin of the people. Nevertheless he wills the Father's will to come to pass, in order that we too might learn that, even if nature draws us the opposite way, we must rather obey God — that is, fulfill his will.[^8]

[^1]: Greek *ἀδημονεῖν* ("to be in anguish, sore distress, anxious dread"), paired with *λυπεῖσθαι* ("to be grieved"): «ἤρξατο λυπεῖσθαι καὶ ἀδημονεῖν». *ἀδημονεῖν* is a strong word for agitated distress and recurs throughout the comment; kept as "be in anguish" / "anguish" for consistency.
[^2]: The lemma runs Matt. 26:37–39. *ὡς σύ* ("as you") has its verb left unexpressed in the Greek; "*will*" is supplied and italicized. «Περίλυπός ἐστιν ἡ ψυχή μου ἕως θανάτου» — "My soul is exceedingly sorrowful (περίλυπος), unto death." Several OCR corruptions in the source are securely restored: «τάτλέγει»→*τότε λέγει*, «Ππεσεν»→*ἔπεσεν*, «προσκυχόμενος»→*προσευχόμενος*, «ἐνὼ θάλω»→*ἐγὼ θέλω*.
[^3]: Greek *οἰκονομικῶς* ("by way of [divine] economy / dispensation / providential arrangement"): «οἰκονομικῶς δὲ λυπεῖται καὶ ἀδημονεῖ». The term marks the grief as deliberately assumed for a saving purpose — here, to prove the reality of Christ's humanity — not as an involuntary passion. OCR «οἰκωνομικῶς» and «πεστώσηται» (→*πιστώσηται*, "confirm / give assurance") are secure.
[^4]: The reasoning: death is *παρὰ φύσιν* ("contrary to nature"), an intruder into a nature created for incorruption; therefore it belongs to that same nature to shrink from (*δειλιᾷν*, "to fear / play the coward before") death. The shrinking is thus natural and blameless.
[^5]: Greek *ψιλὸς ἄνθρωπος* ("a mere / bare man," a man and nothing more): «ἐπιπηδήσῃ αὐτῷ ὡς ψιλῷ ἀνθρώπῳ». This is the classic "deception of the devil" motif: the devil, taking Christ for a mere man, "leaps upon" (*ἐπιπηδάω*) him to kill him, and so is himself "crushed" (*συντριβῇ*) by attacking one he could not hold. OCR «ἐπιπηδήοῃ» is *ἐπιπηδήσῃ*.
[^6]: I.e. if Christ had eagerly rushed toward (*ἐπεπήδησε*, "leapt upon") his own death, the Jews could have pleaded that in killing "one who leapt upon the passion" they did no wrong, since he sought it himself. By shrinking from it, he leaves them without excuse. OCR «σος Ἐβραίους» is *τοὺς Ἑβραίους*.
[^7]: Theophylact offers four reasons why the passion is called "a cup" (*ποτήριον*): (1) the "sleep" it brings — death as sleep, and a drink as sleep-inducing; (2) it is a *σωτήριον* ("salvific / saving") cause of gladness; (3) to show that as man he naturally declines (*παραιτεῖται*, "begs off, asks to be excused from") death; (4) that he did not wish the Hebrews to incur so ruinous a guilt. The first reason («διὰ τὸν ἐκ τούτου ὕπνον») is compressed and elliptical; the sense is that a cup, like death, brings on sleep.
[^8]: The final clause is elliptical: «δεῖ πείθεσθαι τῷ Θεῷ μᾶλλον, ἢ τὸ ἐκείνου θέλημα πληροῦν». The *ἢ* is taken here as epexegetic ("that is") rather than a true comparative ("rather than"): "we must rather obey God — that is, fulfill his will." A comparative reading ("obey God rather than fulfill his [own/nature's] will") is grammatically possible but jars with the sense, since fulfilling God's will is precisely the thing commended. OCR «πατοικὸν»→*πατρικὸν* ("of the Father / paternal"), «σωσοτήριον»→*σωτήριον*.
