# Matthew 26 (ΚϚʹ) — Lemma 38 · Matt. 26:40b–41

> **… to watch with me? Watch and pray, that you enter not into temptation. The spirit on the one hand *is* willing, but the flesh *is* weak. (Matt. 26:41)**[^1]

Since Peter was bold, and the other disciples *also*, he reproves their weakness, as *of men* who had spoken rather rashly;[^2] and most of all he converses with Peter. "So, were you not able *for* one hour to keep awake with me? And how *then* will you lay down your souls[^3] for me?" But again, since through the reproof he had struck *them*, he heals *them* in saying that the spirit on the one hand *is* willing, but the weakness of the flesh resists — that is, "I deem you worthy of pardon, because not through contempt did you doze off, but through weakness; so that henceforth, since you are weak, do not be bold, but pray that you enter not into temptation at all."

But others understand the *words* "that you enter not into temptation" as *equivalent* to "that you be not overcome by the temptation." For surely, they say, he does not command us to be untried;[^4] for the temptations are the *very things* that bring the crown.[^5] Rather, the not being swallowed up by the temptation, and *not* entering into its belly as *into that* of some wild beast — this he commands *us* to pray *for*; for the *one* defeated by the temptation entered into it, that is, was swallowed up by it,[^6]

[^1]: The lemma is continuous with the preceding one: it opens with the tail of Matt. 26:40 («γρηγορῆσαι μετ᾽ ἐμοῦ;» — "to watch with me?"), then gives 26:41 in full. The μὲν … δὲ antithesis («τὸ μὲν πνεῦμα πρόθυμον, ἡ δὲ σὰρξ ἀσθενής») is rendered "on the one hand … but"; a supplied "*is*" carries the verbless Greek predications.
[^2]: Greek *ὡς προπετέστερα φθεγξαμένων* — "as *ones* having uttered *things* rather rashly" (comparative *προπετέστερα*, "too/rather headlong"); referring to the disciples' earlier boast that they would die with him (Matt. 26:35). *θρασύς* just above is rendered "bold" in the sense of "rash, over-confident."
[^3]: Greek *τὰς ψυχὰς ὑμῶν … θήσετε*: *ψυχή* is "soul" or "life"; the idiom "lay down *the* soul/life" (cf. John 10:11) here echoes the disciples' pledge to die for him. "Souls" is kept for transparency, "lives" being the equivalent idiom.
[^4]: Greek *ἀπειράστους* — "untempted, not put to trial." The point is that trials as such are not to be prayed away.
[^5]: Greek *οἱ πειρασμοὶ … εἰσιν οἱ στεφανοῦντες* — literally "the temptations are the *ones that* crown," i.e. that win *us* the victor's crown. Supplied "very" and "bring the" render the substantival participle idiomatically.
[^6]: The Greek syntax is loose: «ἀλλὰ τὸ μὴ καταποθῆναι ὑπὸ τοῦ πειρασμοῦ, καὶ εἰσελθεῖν εἰς τὴν κοιλίαν αὐτοῦ ὡς θηρίου τινὸς, τοῦτο κελεύει εὔχεσθαι.» The negation *μὴ* governs *καταποθῆναι* ("be swallowed up"); the following infinitive *εἰσελθεῖν* ("to enter") stands in apposition to it, so the whole is what one prays *against*. Rendered with a supplied "*not*" before "entering" to make the shared negation explicit. The exposition then breaks off mid-sentence after «κατεπόθη ὑπ᾽ αὐτοῦ» ("was swallowed up by it,"); the next lemma resumes with a fresh scripture quotation rather than completing the thought.
