# Matthew 22 (ΚΒʹ) — Lemma 7 · Matt. 22:7

> **And when that king heard *it*, he was angered, and having sent his armies, he destroyed those murderers, and burned their city. (Matt. 22:7)**[^1]

This parable too intimates the disobedience of the Jews, just as also *does* that of the vineyard. Except in so far as that one, on the one hand, indicates the death of Christ, but this one, on the other hand, a wedding joy, that is, the resurrection. And this one shows that these sinned worse than those in the former parable. For those, on the one hand, being asked for fruits, killed those who asked; but these, on the other hand, being called to a wedding, displayed *their* murderousness.

Therefore God is likened to a human king; for he does not appear such as he is, but such as he must appear toward us. When, on the one hand, therefore, we die as men, being subject to human failings, God appears to us as a man; but when, on the other hand, we walk as gods, then God stands in *the* assembly of gods.[^2] Just as, therefore, also when we live as beasts it comes to pass; and he *becomes* for us a panther, and a bear, and a lion.[^3] And he makes a wedding for his Son, joining him to every beautiful soul. For the bridegroom is Christ, but the bride is the Church and the soul.

And the servants sent first are those about Moses, whom the Hebrews did not obey, but provoked God in the wilderness forty years,[^4] and they were not willing to receive the word of God and the spiritual joy. Then other servants were sent, the prophets. But of these also, some, on the one hand, they killed, as Isaiah; but others, on the other hand, they insulted, as Jeremiah, casting *him* into a pit of mire.[^5] But the more moderate ones excused themselves: the one, on the one hand, having gone away to his own field, that is, having turned aside to a flesh-loving and luxurious life — for the field proper to each is the body; but the other, to his merchandise, that is, to the gain-loving life — for merchants are a most gain-loving race. The parable therefore shows that those who fail of the spiritual wedding, and of the union with Christ and the banqueting, fail for the most part on account of these two things: either on account of bodily pleasures, or on account of the passion of gain-loving.

And here he names *it* a "breakfast" (*ἄριστον*), although elsewhere calling it a "supper" (*δεῖπνον*) — not without reason.[^6] For a "supper," on the one hand, inasmuch as such a wedding[^7] appeared perfectly in the last times, and toward the evening, that is, the end of the ages; but a "breakfast," on the other hand, because also in the former ages the mystery was being revealed, even if more dimly. And the bulls and the fatlings *are* the Old and the New Covenant. For the Old, on the one hand, is signified through the bulls, for it had animal-sacrifices; but the New, through the fatlings; for we now offer loaves at the altar, which would more properly be called "fatlings" (*σιτιστά*), as being composed of grain (*σῖτος*).[^8] God accordingly calls us that we may eat both the good things of the Old Scripture and those of the New. But also, when you see someone interpreting the divine oracles clearly, know that such a one gives "fatling" foods; for he who teaches clearly, as it were, feeds with grain and fills with food the simpler ones. And here you will inquire how he says,[^9]

[^1]: The lemma is continuous with the preceding lemma (06), which breaks off at «Καὶ» ("And"); its opening word here is *ἀκούσας*. Full Greek: «Καὶ ἀκούσας ὁ βασιλεὺς ἐκεῖνος, ὠργίσθη, καὶ πέμψας τὰ στρατεύματα αὐτοῦ, ἀπώλεσε τοὺς φονεῖς ἐκείνους, καὶ τὴν πόλιν αὐτῶν ἐνέπρησεν.» The supplied "*it*" (object of "heard") is not in the Greek.
[^2]: An allusion to Ps. 81:1 LXX (82:1 MT): «ὁ Θεὸς ἔστη ἐν συναγωγῇ θεῶν» ("God stood in *the* assembly of gods"). Theophylact reads the "gods" as deified human beings.
[^3]: An allusion to Hos. 13:7–8 LXX, where God says he will be to *them* «ὡς πανθὴρ» ("as a panther/leopard"), «ὡς ἄρκος» ("as a bear"), and like a lion — beasts of judgment answering to those who live as beasts.
[^4]: Cf. Ps. 94:10 LXX (95:10 MT) and Num. 14:33–34; the Greek *παρεπίκραναν* ("they provoked/embittered") echoes the wilderness "provocation."
[^5]: For Jeremiah cast into the miry pit, Jer. 38:6 (LXX 45:6): «εἰς λάκκον βορβόρου». Isaiah's being killed reflects the tradition that he was sawn asunder (cf. Heb. 11:37).
[^6]: The wordplay turns on two words for a meal: *ἄριστον* (the earlier/midday meal, here "breakfast") versus *δεῖπνον* (the evening meal, "supper"), the latter being the term used in the parallel parable at Luke 14:16 («δεῖπνον μέγα»). Theophylact exploits the temporal connotations of each.
[^7]: OCR reads «τοῦ τοιούτου άΚμου», corrupt; the sense requires a noun for the feast/wedding, most plausibly *γάμου* ("wedding") — the subject throughout — hence "such a wedding." Reading uncertain; flagged.
[^8]: A second wordplay: *σιτιστά* ("fatted/grain-fed animals," the "fatlings" of Matt. 22:4) is derived by Theophylact from *σῖτος* ("grain, wheat"), so that grain-loaves are "more etymologically" (*ἐτυμώτερον*) the true "fatlings." The same root recurs just below in *σιτίζει* ("feeds with grain").
[^9]: The sentence breaks off and is completed in the following lemma (08), which supplies the words «Καλέσατε τοὺς κεκλημένους» ("Call those who have been called").
