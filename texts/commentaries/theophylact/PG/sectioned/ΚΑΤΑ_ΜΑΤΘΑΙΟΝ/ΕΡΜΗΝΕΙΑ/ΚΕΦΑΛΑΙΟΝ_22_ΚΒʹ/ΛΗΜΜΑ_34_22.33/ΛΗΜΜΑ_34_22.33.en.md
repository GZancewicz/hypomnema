# Matthew 22 (ΚΒʹ) — Lemma 34 · Matt. 22:33 (expounding 22:29–33)

> **…having heard, the crowds were astonished at his teaching. (Matt. 22:33)**

The Savior shows that both there will be a resurrection, and *that it will* not *be* such a fleshly *one* as they are deceived *into imagining*,[^1] but *one* more divine and more spiritual. For why, he says, are you deceived, not knowing the Scriptures nor the power of God? For if, on the one hand, you knew the Scriptures, you would understand that God is not a God of *the* dead, but of *the* living; but if, on the other hand, you knew the power of God, you would know that to God all things *are* possible, so as even to make men live as citizens *of heaven*, as *the* angels *do*.[^2]

But behold the wisdom of the Lord: those *men* were eager, from Moses, to overturn the doctrine of the resurrection, and he, from Moses again, persuades them. "For I am," he says, "the God of Abraham, and of Isaac, and of Jacob."[^3] And what he says is this: God is not a God of those *who are* not, but of those *who* subsist and are;[^4] for neither did he say, "I *was*," but, "I *am*." For even if they died, yet by the hope of the resurrection they live.

But you ask: How then does he elsewhere say that he is Lord both of *the* dead and of *the* living?[^5] Learn, then, that by "dead" there he means those who, on the one hand, have died, but, on the other hand, are about to live. But here the Lord, taking his stand against the heresy of the Sadducees — who dogmatize that the soul is not immortal but is utterly destroyed — says that God is not a God of *the* dead, that is, of those who, as it seems to you, are completely destroyed, but of *the* living, that is, of those who have the soul immortal and who will rise again, even if now they are dead.

[^1]: Greek *οὐ τοιαύτη σαρκικὴ ὡς πλανῶνται* — "not such a fleshly *one* as they are led astray [into thinking]." The verb *πλανῶνται* ("they are deceived / led astray") is the same used of the Sadducees in the lemma («You are deceived, *πλανᾶσθε*»); the Savior grants there is a resurrection but denies it is the crudely fleshly one the Sadducees imagine in order to ridicule it.
[^2]: Greek *ὥστε καὶ τοὺς ἀνθρώπους ὡς ἀγγέλους ποιῆσαι πολιτεύεσθαι*. The verb *πολιτεύεσθαι* means "to live as a citizen, to conduct one's life *in a commonwealth*"; it has no clean English equivalent that keeps the political metaphor. Rendered "to live as citizens *of heaven*, as *the* angels *do*"; a plainer rendering is "so as to make even men conduct their manner of life like angels."
[^3]: Exodus 3:6, quoted (as in the lemma) with the Lord's own words as the ground of the resurrection.
[^4]: Greek *οὐκ ἔστι τῶν μὴ ὄντων Θεός, ἀλλὰ τῶν ὑφισταμένων καὶ ὄντων* — "not a God of the non-existent, but of those subsisting and existing." *ὑφισταμένων* ("subsisting, having real substance/existence") is a weightier term than the following *ὄντων* ("being, existing"); the pair insists the patriarchs still have real subsistence, not mere past existence.
[^5]: A reference to Rom. 14:9, *ἵνα καὶ νεκρῶν καὶ ζώντων κυριεύσῃ* ("that he might be Lord both of *the* dead and of *the* living"). Theophylact reconciles the two: in Paul "dead" means the deceased who will yet live, whereas here "dead" is used in the Sadducees' sense of the annihilated — of whom God is precisely *not* the God.
