# Matthew 13 (ΙΓʹ) — Lemma 40 · Matt. 13:56–57

> **…sisters, are they not all with us?[^1] Whence therefore to this *man* all these things? And they were made to stumble[^2] in him. (Matt. 13:56–57)**

The Nazarenes, being senseless, supposed that lowness of birth[^3] and the meanness of *one's* forebears harms a person with respect to being well-pleasing to God. For let it be granted that Jesus was a mere man,[^4] and not God: what was hindering him from becoming great in wonders? They are found, therefore, both senseless and envious. For they ought rather to have prided themselves[^5] that their native place had brought forth such a good *thing*.

Now the Lord had brothers and sisters, the children of Joseph, whom he begot from the wife of his brother Clopas.[^6] For, Clopas having died childless, Joseph took his wife according to the law, and procreated from her six children, four males and two females — Mary, who was called the daughter of Clopas according to the law, and Salome. And the *phrase* "they are with us" *stands* in place of "they dwell here among us." These too, therefore, were made to stumble in Jesus, perhaps saying, they also, that "by Beelzebul he casts out the demons."[^7]

[^1]: Greek *πρὸς ἡμᾶς εἰσι* — literally "are toward/with us." Theophylact himself glosses the phrase below as *μεθ᾽ ἡμῶν ὧδε κατοικοῦσιν* ("they dwell here among us"), i.e. they are our neighbors and townsfolk. The lemma continues the Gospel quotation begun in the previous section, opening mid-word at *ἀδελφαί* ("sisters").
[^2]: Greek *ἐσκανδαλίζοντο* ("they were scandalized / made to stumble / caused to take offense"), the verb of *σκάνδαλον*. Rendered "were made to stumble" both in the lemma and in the exposition below to keep the term visible; "were offended" is the common alternative.
[^3]: Greek *δυσγένεια* ("low or ignoble birth, base descent"), paired with *εὐτέλεια* ("cheapness, meanness, insignificance"). The two nouns together denote humble and undistinguished ancestry.
[^4]: Greek *ἄνθρωπος ψιλός* ("a bare / mere man"), the standard term for a merely human being as opposed to God — the very error Theophylact is rebutting. "Let it be granted" renders the concessive *Ἔστω γάρ* ("for let it be *so*").
[^5]: Greek *ἐγκαλλωπίζεσθαι* ("to plume oneself, take pride in, glory in"); the middle voice of "beautifying oneself with" something. They should have made their townsman their boast.
[^6]: On the brethren of the Lord, Theophylact follows the tradition that Joseph, by a levirate marriage to the widow of his childless brother Clopas (Greek *Κλωπᾶς*), fathered six children who are reckoned Jesus' "brothers and sisters." *ἔτεκεν* / *ἐπαιδοποίησεν* both denote begetting/procreating offspring; here of Joseph. Mary is styled "daughter of Clopas *according to the law*" (*κατὰ τὸν νόμον*), i.e. legally reckoned to the deceased Clopas by the levirate statute (cf. Deut. 25:5–6), though physically Joseph's.
[^7]: Cf. Matt. 12:24. The point: even his own kin joined in the slander that his exorcisms were worked by the prince of the demons.
