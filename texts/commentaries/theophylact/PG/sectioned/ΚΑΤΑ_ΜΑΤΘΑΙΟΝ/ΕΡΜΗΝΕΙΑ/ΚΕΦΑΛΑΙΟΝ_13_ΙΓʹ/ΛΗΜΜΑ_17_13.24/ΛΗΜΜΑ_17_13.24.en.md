# Matthew 13 (ΙΓʹ) — Lemma 17 · Matt. 13:31-32[^1]

> **Another parable he set before them, saying: The kingdom of the heavens is like a grain of mustard, which a man, having taken, sowed in his field; which, on the one hand, is smaller than all the seeds. But when it is grown, it is greater than all the herbs, and becomes a tree, so that the birds of the heaven come and lodge in its branches. (Matt. 13:31-32)**

The grain of mustard, *is* the preaching and the apostles; for these, seeming few, embraced the whole inhabited world; so that the birds of the heaven — that is, those having a light and winged disposition toward the things above — rest in them. And you, accordingly, be a grain of mustard: small, on the one hand, in appearance (for one must not make a spectacle of virtue),[^2] but fervent and zealous and pungent[^3] and given to reproving; for thus you will become greater than the herbs — that is, than the weak and not-perfect — being yourself perfect, so that the birds of the heaven also — that is, the angels — rest in you, *since you are* living an angelic life. For these also rejoice over the righteous.

[^1]: The folder name records the draws-from verse as 13.24, but the Greek here is the mustard-seed parable, Matt. 13:31-32; the header verse is corrected accordingly.
[^2]: Greek *οὐ γὰρ δεῖ θεατρίζειν τὴν ἀρετήν* — "for one must not put virtue on stage / make a theatrical display of it" (from *θέατρον*, "theater"). Virtue should be small and unshowy in outward appearance.
[^3]: Greek *δριμύς* means literally "pungent, acrid, sharp-tasting" — the natural property of mustard, here transferred to the person: keen, sharp, biting (paired with *ἐλεγκτικός*, "given to reproving / apt to convict"). The mustard-flavor pun ("hot… pungent") is lost in English; *θερμός* just before ("hot, fervent") carries the same double sense of temperature and zeal.
